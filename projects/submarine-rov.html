Eight-Thruster Underwater Inspection ROV

Turning an existing CAD design into a tethered, ArduSub-based ROV for underwater structure and ship-hull inspection.

System Integration Eight-Thruster 6-DOF ArduSub + BlueOS Ongoing Development

Overview

This tethered ROV is being developed for underwater structural inspection, with ship-hull inspection as its main application. It uses a conventional eight-thruster, six-degree-of-freedom architecture inspired by established Blue Robotics-style vehicles.

A previous version experienced stability problems, so the current vehicle is larger and follows a more proven thruster layout. The primary CAD design was completed before I joined the project. Because no physical vehicle was available, my work focused on turning that existing design into an assembled, wired, configured, and test-ready system.

My Role

I worked independently as a UAV Engineering Intern, with guidance from three engineers involved in the previous version. I owned the practical system-integration work needed to move from an existing CAD model to a physical ROV.

  • Reviewed the existing CAD design, identified required hardware, and coordinated CNC manufacture of the HDPE frame parts with an overseas supplier.
  • Communicated dimensions, materials, and manufacturing requirements, then checked and assembled the manufactured frame.
  • Installed the pressure housings and thrusters, and designed the missing battery holder and adjustable retaining ring.
  • Installed and wired the flight controller, companion computer, PWM breakout boards, ESCs, camera servo, lights, camera, and associated connections.
  • Configured BlueOS, ArduSub, tethered Ethernet, QGroundControl, and the surface game controller.
  • Tested all eight thrusters individually, built a simple tether-management rig, and prepared the system for waterproofing, buoyancy work, and underwater testing.

From CAD to Physical Hardware

The project began with a completed CAD model, not a complete vehicle. I reviewed the design to identify the frame material, structural hardware, pressure housings, propulsion components, electronics, seals, cable-routing parts, and missing retention hardware required for assembly.

I coordinated CNC manufacture of the HDPE frame components with an overseas supplier, including the critical dimensions, material requirements, and manufacturing details. Once the parts arrived, I checked their fit, assembled the frame with its metal structural connectors, and installed the two pressure housings and eight thrusters.

The battery installation still needed purpose-built retention hardware, so I designed a battery holder and an adjustable retaining ring for the lower chamber. These parts turned the existing frame design into a practical assembly that could securely package the selected battery.

Mechanical Design

The approximately 38 × 36 × 24 cm vehicle uses a CNC-cut HDPE frame joined by metal structural connectors. The larger, conventional layout provides mounting space for eight thrusters and separates the electronics and battery into two pressure housings.

Upper Housing

Contains the avionics and communication electronics.

Lower Housing

Contains the 4S 5200 mAh LiPo battery and custom retention hardware.

Sealing

O-ring-sealed housings use WetLink components for waterproof cable routing.

Buoyancy and Trim

Buoyancy foam has not yet been installed. During water testing, foam and ballast will be adjusted to bring the ROV close to neutral buoyancy while keeping the center of buoyancy above the center of mass. This arrangement is intended to provide a restoring tendency and improve vehicle stability.

Propulsion and 6-DOF Control

Eight ROVMaker 2216 underwater brushless thrusters are arranged using ArduSub’s standard eight-thruster, six-degree-of-freedom frame configuration. The intended control axes are:

SurgeForward and backward translation
SwayLeft and right translation
HeaveUpward and downward translation
RollRotation about the longitudinal axis
PitchNose-up and nose-down rotation
YawLeft and right heading rotation

Testing boundary: all eight thrusters have been tested individually. Coordinated underwater movement across these six axes has not yet been tested.

Electronics and Software

A Pixhawk 4 running ArduSub manages stabilization, pilot commands, thruster outputs, and accessories. A Raspberry Pi 4 running BlueOS serves as the onboard companion computer and connects to the Pixhawk over USB.

I installed and wired the Pixhawk, Raspberry Pi, PWM breakout boards, electronic speed controllers, camera tilt servo, underwater lights, USB inspection camera, Ethernet hardware, and related power and signal connections. I then installed BlueOS, configured ArduSub, set up QGroundControl, mapped the Fantech controller, and verified each thruster output individually.

Tethered Communications

The ROV uses a topside and onboard pair of Ethernet-over-two-wire interfaces to carry network traffic through the tether. On the surface, a USB-to-Ethernet adapter connects the interface to the laptop. Onboard, the second interface restores Ethernet for the Raspberry Pi 4.

The surface laptop uses the static IP address 192.168.2.1. This network provides live camera video, MAVLink telemetry, QGroundControl commands, BlueOS access, and vehicle configuration and status data through a single tether connection.

I established the full link, configured the Raspberry Pi and topside networking, verified BlueOS access, and built a simple rig to manage the tether during future testing.

Camera and Lighting

A USB inspection camera provides the live video feed through BlueOS. It is mounted on a one-axis servo-actuated tilt mechanism so the operator can adjust the viewing angle during inspection.

Two controllable underwater lights support inspection in low-visibility areas. Their controls provide on/off operation and brightness adjustment from the surface interface. Underwater image quality, camera movement, and lighting performance still need to be validated during water trials.

Engineering Challenges

Compact Wiring and Electronics Packaging

Power, PWM, USB, Ethernet, camera, lighting, servo, and thruster connections all had to fit inside a small enclosure. The wiring needed to avoid pinching, remain traceable for troubleshooting, and still allow the housing to close and seal correctly.

Turning CAD into Hardware

The original frame design was already complete, but the physical vehicle was not. I had to identify the components, coordinate manufacturing, assemble the structure, and design the missing battery-retention parts.

Tethered Networking

The communication chain required onboard and topside conversion interfaces, Ethernet configuration, Raspberry Pi networking, BlueOS, and QGroundControl to work as one system.

Battery Packaging

The battery chamber restricted the allowable battery dimensions. That constraint influenced the selection of the 4S 5200 mAh LiPo and required a custom holder and adjustable retaining ring.

Preparing for Underwater Testing

Testing must progress methodically through seal checks, leak testing, buoyancy trimming, shallow-water trials, and coordinated motion testing before the ROV can be used for a ship-hull inspection demonstration.

Current Status and Testing Roadmap

The vehicle is mechanically assembled, integrated, and communicating over its tether. Underwater testing has not yet taken place.

Reflection

This project reinforced that system integration is the work of turning many individually reasonable components into one understandable, serviceable vehicle. Starting from an existing CAD design required me to bridge manufacturing, mechanical assembly, electronics packaging, networking, software configuration, and testing preparation.

The next milestone is not simply putting the ROV in water. It is building confidence in stages: prove the seals, establish buoyancy and trim, validate accessories, then test coordinated motion before attempting an inspection mission.
